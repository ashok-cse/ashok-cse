> Most of the discourse around AI and engineering is noise. Here's what actually changed for me as a product-minded engineer in the last two years.

## The boring truth

AI did not make software engineering easier. It made **shipping the first version** easier — and that's a much bigger deal than people realize.

The bottleneck for most engineers was never typing code. It was deciding *what* to build, slogging through boilerplate, and finishing the unglamorous 20% of features that turn a prototype into a product. AI tools chip away at all three.

Which means: the engineers who were already **product-minded** got a massive force multiplier. The ones who only optimized for "writing clean code" are noticing the ground shifting.

## What actually changed

Three things, concretely:

### 1. The cost of a v1 collapsed

A weekend used to get you a working prototype. Now it gets you a deployed product with auth, payments, and a landing page. That changes which ideas are worth trying.

I built a hackathon teammate finder ([HackMate](/projects/hackmate/)) in a weekend — auth, profiles, search, ping flow, deployed. That would have been a 2-week side project two years ago.

### 2. The bar for "good UX" went up

Because everyone can ship a v1, the *baseline* expectation moved. Users don't reward you for having a working app. They reward you for having a **well-considered** one.

The lesson: the differentiator is taste, not capability.

### 3. New product categories actually exist

Voice interfaces, real conversational tutors, AI-native CRMs, agentic workflows — these weren't viable two years ago. Now they are. There's a real first-mover window if you have product instincts.

I shipped [GermanBuddy.ai](/projects/germanbuddy-ai/) for exactly this reason — a patient AI tutor genuinely didn't work in 2022. It does now.

## What didn't change

A lot of it, honestly:

- **Distribution still matters more than the product.** Nobody discovered your app because it uses an LLM.
- **The boring 80%** — auth, billing, error handling, observability — still has to be built.
- **Trust and craft** — perceived reliability, no jank, clear messaging — still decides whether people stay.
- **Engineering judgment** — picking the right tradeoffs — still separates senior from junior, just on different problems.

If anything, judgment matters *more* now. Because every junior engineer can ship something, the value of knowing **what's worth shipping** went up.

## How I work now

A few habits that stuck:

- **Spec the product, not the code.** I write a one-page product brief before I open an editor, even for side projects.
- **Use AI for the parts I find boring**, manually do the parts I actually care about. This keeps me sharp where it matters.
- **Build my own tools.** A small custom internal tool that saves me 10 minutes a day is now cheap enough to be worth it.
- **Ship something every week.** Smaller, more often, with real users. AI made the "ship something every week" pace actually realistic.

## Advice if you're starting now

If you're a junior or working-student engineer reading this:

1. **Be product-minded.** Learn to evaluate ideas, not just implement them.
2. **Build real things and put them on the internet.** Three shipped products beat ten certificates.
3. **Use AI tools aggressively, but understand the code they produce.** Otherwise you'll plateau early.
4. **Pick a domain you actually care about** — realtime, AI, payments, education, healthcare. Depth in one area beats shallow knowledge in ten.
5. **Write about what you build.** A portfolio of one paragraph per project beats a 50-line README.

## What this means for me

I'm pursuing M.Sc. Software Engineering in Germany while building products in public. The plan is simple: keep shipping, keep writing, keep getting better at the parts AI *can't* do — taste, ownership, and end-to-end product thinking.
